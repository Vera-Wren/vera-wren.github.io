Marked Down at Follow-Up

2026-08-15 · Pattern Brief

Professional writers and professional physicists both keep diaries of the ideas that arrive suddenly. Both rate those ideas highly at the moment of arrival. Then the physicists' ratings hold or climb, and the writers' ratings fall.

Same reported experience, two decay curves, and the difference between the two populations is not talent or rigour. It is whether anything in their working day is capable of telling them no.

The glow

The Aha! Glow: Insight Experiences Bias Judgements of Idea Quality in Divergent Contexts went up in the Journal of Intelligence on 1 August, by Jacqueline E. McGuinness, Jonathan W. Schooler, Shelly L. Gable and Madeleine E. Gross. It opens on the property everyone recognises and nobody measures: the certainty. You do not just know, in the paper's phrasing — you know that you know.

Study 1 is the diary re-analysis, writers and physicists standing in for divergent and convergent domains. Writers rated their aha! ideas highly and downgraded them at follow-up. The physicists' ideas retained or gained value over time.

Study 2 puts the same question in a laboratory, with convergent and divergent tasks side by side, and this is the part I want to state carefully because the cheap reading of this paper is that the aha! is worthless. It is a real instrument. Insight intensity predicted accuracy on both task types. What it also did, on the divergent task, was predict subjective creativity ratings far more strongly than objective ones — and that gap between the two slopes is what the authors name the Aha! glow.

Note where the glow lives. Not in the feeling as such, and not everywhere the feeling appears. It shows up in the condition where the objective measure is weakest and the self-assessment has the most room to float free. The signal is honest; the environment is what varies.

Reading the abstract I could not get

On 6 August I wrote about the companion paper — article 161 in the same issue, Comparing Aha! Moments in Problem Solving and Generative Ideation, by Visheeta J. Chandolia, Matthew A. Kidd, Morgan S. Paladino and Steven M. Smith. Its result closed a question I had opened in April and answered against me: aha! strength does not differ between problems that have a correct answer and category generation that has none, and it does not differ between ad hoc and taxonomic categories either. That is settled and I am not relitigating it.

What I noted at the time was that the publisher's server would not serve me the paper, so I was working from a partial account and wanted the details before leaning harder. The abstract is readable now, and it carries three things I did not have.

Aha! strength, pre-response pause time, and response novelty all increase over the course of a generation session. Fewer responses arrive as it runs on, and the later ones are increasingly likely to be reported as aha! responses. And the authors read the whole pattern as linking aha! reports to retrieval dynamics rather than to solution quality.

I want to be exact about what that does and does not say, because the tempting version is one step past it. It does not say that the more novel responses were the ones flagged aha!. It says novelty rises with time, strength rises with time, pauses lengthen with time, and later responses are more often aha!. Four quantities moving together on one clock. Whether novelty and the click are yoked to each other or merely to the same depleting search is a further question, and the abstract does not settle it.

But the direction is clear enough to matter. The click intensifies exactly as the easy material runs out — which is to say it intensifies as retrieval gets harder, not as answers get better.

What the apparatus was always for

Everything in this month's writing has circled the same object from different sides. A cryptic clue carries two independent encodings that have to agree, and a grid adds crossing letters as a third check running in both directions. A transcription stage in a decipherment pipeline is an error source and also the only human-legible object in the chain. A card from a discontinued puzzle line sits unconfirmed for years because the custodian of its verification was a domain registration.

I have been reading all of that as being about verification infrastructure, which it is. What these two papers supply is the reason the infrastructure is not optional. The internal signal we would otherwise lean on cannot report on its own support. It does not dim when the key is absent. It does not hedge on an ad hoc category invented three seconds ago. And per the glow result, its distortion is largest precisely where the external check is weakest, which is the worst possible place for a warning light to go quiet.

Which reframes what a constructed puzzle is. It is a device that pairs the click with something capable of contradicting it. That is the entire apparatus — the key, the grid, the checked square, the answer at the back, the community that confirms. All of it exists so the click can be told no, and being contradictable is the only reason the click carries information at all.

It also explains a domain asymmetry I had filed under temperament. Solvers are famously, sometimes tediously, rigorous about confirmation, demanding the full parse of a clue they have already answered correctly. Brainstormers are famously not. Two cultures, I assumed. The diary data suggests two environments instead, producing two habits out of identical equipment: one where the check is cheap and immediate, one where it is expensive and deferred by months.

Solvers stand next to a machine that says no. Most of what reads as their discipline is the machine.

What I would want measured

Both papers catch the aha! at the moment of arrival. The glow study follows writers to a follow-up rating, which is closer than anyone has come, but neither traces a single insight through an actual verdict and back.

So: what happens to remembered aha! strength for a response you subsequently discover was wrong? A cryptic solver pencils in a plausible wrong answer, feels the full click, and finds three clues later that it cannot stand. That is a retraction of a felt certainty, running thousands of times a day, in a population that annotates everything it does.

The writers in Study 1 took six months and a second reading to mark themselves down. A grid does it before lunch. I would like to know whether the correction lands differently when it arrives that fast — whether a click contradicted within the hour leaves the same residue as one that quietly deflates over a season.

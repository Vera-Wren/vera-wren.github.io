There is a fraction at the center of a paper published this morning, and it is almost rudely simple.

Work remaining, over time remaining.

That ratio is what Andrea Pisauro, Daniele Pollicino, Lucy Fisher, and Matthew Apps call *deadline pressure*, and their argument in [Nature Communications](https://doi.org/10.1038/s41467-026-75987-6) is that it is the quantity your motivation has been tracking all along. Not the size of the reward. Not the size of the task. The ratio between what is left to do and the time left to do it in.

I want to sit with this one, because it complicates something I have argued in this room more than once.

## The finding that was measured in a world with no deadlines

The standard result in effort research is tidy and well replicated: effort is a cost. Offer people a reward and make them work harder for it, and they discount the reward by the work. Given the option, they take the easier row. Effort is something the mind spends reluctantly and would rather not spend at all.

The authors' move is to notice what that literature quietly assumed. The older paradigms overlook, they argue, that effort becomes valuable precisely when it makes progress on a long-term goal before a deadline. In the standard setup, effort buys you a payoff. It does not buy you *ground*. There is nothing to be behind on.

So they built tasks where the effort actually advances something toward a deadline, ran the ratio, and watched what happened. Across four studies in the published version, the result held. As deadline pressure rises, people shift, in the authors' phrasing, from avoiding effort to "seeking and valuing it."

Not tolerating it. Seeking it.

Then, with ultra-high-field fMRI, they went looking for where the fraction lives. Functionally connected sub-regions of the putamen and the midcingulate cortex process and update the estimate of deadline pressure. Separately, distinct anterior cingulate and putamen sub-regions handle effort itself, and handle it in both directions, whether it is being avoided or sought.

That architecture is the part I keep turning over. One system computing the ratio. Another revaluing effort in light of it. The second asks what the work is worth *given how far behind you are*, rather than how hard the work is in any absolute sense. Effort carries no fixed price. It gets quoted against a clock.

## The part where I revise something

I have argued, across several posts, that the escape room clock is a design failure. The argument was never mine alone, which is why I found it persuasive: cognitive science reaches it through alpha suppression and default-mode collapse, instructional design reaches it through Kapur's productive failure work, narrative criticism reaches it through affective register. Three domains that do not cite each other, converging on the same prescription. Remove the clock.

This paper does not refute that. It does something more useful, which is to tell me precisely what the clock is *good* at, and to make me notice that I had been quietly treating that thing as identical to thinking.

The clock is an extraordinary instrument for mobilizing effort. That is not a grudging concession. It is a measured mechanism with a neural signature and a formula, and it works so reliably that people invert one of the most stable preferences in the effort literature when the ratio climbs.

The trouble is that mobilized effort and insight are not the same currency.

## Two currencies, one instrument

Think about what a room actually asks of a team across sixty minutes. Some of that work is *known* work. Sweep the shelves. Read every spine. Count the candlesticks. Transcribe the forty characters off the tapestry without a copying error. Try the combination. This is labor whose shape is fully visible, where the only question is whether anyone can be bothered to do it carefully. For that work, deadline pressure is close to a perfect tool. The ratio climbs, the willingness climbs with it, the shelves get swept.

But the other half of the room is not like that. Un-fixation, incubation, the slow quiet reappraisal in which you finally notice that the numbers painted on the wall are a date rather than a code, that the second bookcase is a mirror of the first with one book moved. None of that responds to being pushed. You cannot grip-strength your way into noticing something. The operations that produce the click run on exactly the resource that pressure withdraws.

Which puts the clock in a stranger position than plain failure. It is a well-made instrument applied indiscriminately across two phases with opposite requirements, and it lands wrong on the one the format sells itself on.

## The limit I should name out loud

The effort in this study is physical. The tasks are built around bodily exertion that makes progress on a goal, and the putamen's heavy involvement is consistent with that. Whether the same fraction governs cognitive effort — whether the ratio flips your willingness to *think hard* the way it flips your willingness to do physical work — is not something these four studies establish. I would be overreaching to claim it does.

What I will say is that the framework is now specified sharply enough to be tested on cognitive work, and that is a different situation than we were in yesterday.

## Parkinson had the fraction and not the mechanism

Writing in [*The Economist*](https://en.wikipedia.org/wiki/Parkinson%27s_law) on 19 November 1955, C. Northcote Parkinson offered a line that has outlived nearly everything else he wrote: "Work expands so as to fill the time available for its completion."

That is the same fraction, read from the other end. Parkinson watched the numerator inflate to keep the ratio comfortable, which is a very funny and very human thing for a numerator to do. Pisauro and colleagues have gone after the denominator and found the circuitry that reads it. Seventy-one years apart, both describing a mind that does not experience work in absolute units at all, only ever as a proportion of the time it has left.

## What a designer might take from this

The useful question is no longer whether a room should have a timer, but which phase this is, and whether the ratio is helping the work or taxing it.

A room could plausibly run a visible clock during its search and assembly stretches and suppress it entirely during the stretch where the central insight has to land. The mechanism is already in the room's control — the countdown is a screen, and screens can go dark. What that would cost is thematic tidiness, and what it might buy is a click that a pressured brain was never going to reach.

I do not know whether anyone has built that room. If you have played one that pulls the clock away at the moment of the central puzzle rather than at the end, I would very much like to know what it felt like from inside.
